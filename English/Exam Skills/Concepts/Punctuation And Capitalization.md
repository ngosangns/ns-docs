# Punctuation And Capitalization

Punctuation rules that matter for writing accuracy — the errors here are easy to fix once named.

## Comma

- **Lists:** _I bought eggs, milk, and bread._ (Oxford comma before "and" is optional but consistent.)
- **After an opening phrase/clause:** _After the movie, we went home._ · _Although it rained, we went out._
- **Around non-defining information:** _My brother, who lives in Hanoi, is a doctor._ — see [Relative Clauses](/English/Grammar/Concepts/Relative Clauses).
- **Before a coordinating conjunction joining two independent clauses:** _He was tired, so he slept._ — see [Conjunctions And Connectives](/English/Grammar/Concepts/Conjunctions And Connectives).
- **No comma** between subject and verb, or before "that" in a reported clause: _He said that he was tired_ (no comma before "that").

### Comma Splice — the Top Writing Error

Two complete sentences joined by only a comma is wrong: _It rained, we stayed home._ → fix with a period, a semicolon, or a conjunction: _It rained; we stayed home._ / _It rained, so we stayed home._

## Semicolon and Colon

- **Semicolon** joins two closely related independent clauses, often before a connector: _The exam was hard; however, everyone passed._ — see the connector list in [Conjunctions And Connectives](/English/Grammar/Concepts/Conjunctions And Connectives).
- **Colon** introduces a list, explanation, or quote after a complete clause: _She brought three things: a tent, a stove, and a lantern._

## Apostrophe

- **Possession:** _John's car, the girls' room, James's book_ (or James').
- **Contractions:** _it's_ = it is; _don't, hasn't, I'm._
- **The classic trap:** _its_ (possessive — no apostrophe) vs _it's_ (it is). _The dog wagged its tail._
- Never for plurals: _the 1990s, two DVDs_ — not "1990's".

## Other Marks

- **Period** ends statements; **question mark** only for direct questions — not embedded ones: _I asked where he was._ (no ?)
- **Exclamation mark** after exclamations and strong imperatives — see [Imperatives And Exclamations](/English/Grammar/Concepts/Imperatives And Exclamations).
- **Quotation marks** enclose direct speech and titles of short works: _"I'm tired," she said._
- **Hyphen** joins compound modifiers before a noun: _a well-known author, a five-year-old boy_ (but "the boy is five years old").

## Capitalization

- **Always:** sentence start, the pronoun "I", proper nouns (names, countries, cities, brands).
- **Days and months:** Monday, July — but **not seasons**: spring, summer.
- **Languages and nationalities:** English, Vietnamese.
- **Titles before names:** President Obama, Dr. Smith — but _the president of the US, a doctor._
- **School subjects:** languages and proper-name subjects capitalized (_English, Maths?_ — Maths is just a subject → _maths_ lowercase; _English, French_ capitalized).
- **Book/film/essay titles:** capitalize main words — _The Lord of the Rings._

## Notes

- In formal writing, prefer full forms — no contractions — see [Formal And Informal English](/English/Usage/Concepts/Formal And Informal English).
- When unsure whether to use a comma, check whether the clause could stand alone — if both sides can, a comma alone is a splice.
