# Ladakh

Leh sits at about 3,500 m (commonly given as 3,524 m). The air is dry, UV is strong, the temperature swings hard between sun and shade, and a pass a short drive away can feel like a different day from town. Ladakh is not a difficult place to reach. It is a poor place to be casual. Clothing, the first days at altitude, and food you will actually eat decide whether the trip feels steady or like a fight with your body.

The preparation notes below come from a guide who lives in Ladakh, rides the roads, and leads Vietnamese groups from about age 9 to guests near 80, including runners, climbers, and ironman athletes. Health points are checked against the [Ladakh Tourism advisory](https://tourism.ladakh.gov.in/advisory) and the [CDC Yellow Book 2026](https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/environmental-hazards-risks/high-altitude-travel-and-altitude-illness.html), both read on 2026-10-06. Where those sources disagree, the difference is spelled out.

## Clothing

A single temperature number does not describe the day. Leh can be bright and warm while the next pass is cold and windy. Nubra, Pangong, Hanle, and the high roads do not share one climate. Pack for several layers of weather in the same day, and treat a forecast as a hint.

Layer, then add or remove pieces:

- A base layer that holds heat.
- A mid layer that insulates.
- An outer shell that blocks wind and weather.
- Light, quick-dry clothes that are easy to move in.

Thin dresses, camisole tops, fashion knits, and heavy dress coats can work for a few photos. They are a bad choice for hours in a vehicle or on a bike. Change into something that looks right for the picture, then change back. On a motorbike, cold wind pulls heat off faster than the air temperature suggests.

Footwear: a solid pair of trainers, trekking shoes, or high-cut boots. Heels, flip-flops, and thin sandals do not belong on the steep, rocky, sandy, uneven ground.

## The body at altitude

The air is dry and the oxygen pressure is lower than at sea level. The body needs time. The first days matter most. On the CDC risk table, a first-night sleeping altitude above 3,400 m is the high band for someone who started below 1,200 m, which is where a flight into Leh lands. Training and fitness do not change that risk. Children are as susceptible as adults. People over 50 have slightly less risk, not a free pass. Being strong, young, or well traveled is not acclimatization.

### What the Leh airport sign says

Leh airport displays a local health-authority notice on preventing acute mountain sickness (AMS):

- **Medication:** acetazolamide 250 mg, twice daily.
  - Start 1-2 days before arrival.
  - Continue for 2-3 days after reaching altitude.
- **Rest:** take it easy for the first 24 hours in Leh; avoid exertion.
- **Food and drink:** drink plenty of water, eat carbohydrate-rich food, and avoid stimulants (alcohol, tobacco, and the like).

Photography is strictly prohibited at Leh airport, so no photo of the original signage exists.

The Ladakh Tourism advisory, read the same day, is stricter on rest and lists the same 250 mg twice-daily acetazolamide line, started 2 days before arrival. That dose is what the local pages tell visitors. It is not the CDC prevention dose for a typical adult. See below.

### The first 48 hours

Ladakh Tourism's advisory says to allow at least 48 hours of acclimatization on arrival in Leh, and not to rush on to higher ground. Its schedule is:

- **Day 1.** Complete rest in Leh. No sightseeing. Drink, and eat light.
- **Day 2.** Short, easy walks around Leh town only. No hard effort.
- **Later.** Nearby sights at a similar altitude, then higher places such as Khardung La and Pangong only after that window.

CDC says to do only mild exercise for the first 48 hours at altitude, and to avoid alcohol for those 48 hours. The airport sign's "first 24 hours" is the shorter message on the wall. Plan the trip around the 48-hour schedule.

The dangerous first day is the one that feels fine. People get off the plane, climb stairs, pose, and talk all day, then that night they have a headache, fatigue, dizziness, nausea, or insomnia. Walk lightly. Talk less. Rest often. Drink steadily. You do not need to lie still. Do not turn the day into a sightseeing marathon. The trip report under [First day in Leh](#first-day-in-leh) is one version of that slower day.

### Water

Do not wait until you are thirsty. Sip through the day. Do not drink a large volume in one go, and do not force fluid past what you tolerate. How much you need depends on your body, the work, the weather, and what you lose.

The Ladakh Tourism advisory tells visitors to drink 3 to 4 litres a day. A later field note says about 2 litres of filtered water a day, because the air is dry and water helps the body adjust. Those are two circulated targets. Sip steadily, and do not force either number. CDC lists dehydration among the things that look like AMS, and lists hyponatremia on that same differential, so both too little and too much water can muddy the picture.

### Acetazolamide

Acetazolamide (Diamox) can prevent AMS and can treat it. Whether to take it, and at what dose, depends on medical history, the itinerary, and other medicines. A doctor should make that call. Do not buy a box and follow a dose that worked for someone else on a previous trip. The right drug, the right schedule, and the right person matter more than taking something "to be safe."

Doses on the record, so they are not mixed up:

| Source | Use | Dose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leh airport sign | Prevention, as posted | 250 mg twice daily, started 1-2 days before arrival, continued 2-3 days after reaching altitude |
| Ladakh Tourism advisory | Prevention, as posted | 250 mg twice daily, started 2 days before arrival |
| CDC Yellow Book 2026 | Prevention of AMS and HACE | 125 mg twice daily; 250 mg twice daily only if body weight is over 100 kg. Children: 1.25 mg/kg every 12 hours, up to 125 mg |
| CDC Yellow Book 2026 | Treatment of AMS | 250 mg twice daily. Children: 2.5 mg/kg every 12 hours, up to 250 mg |

The local 250 mg prevention line matches CDC's treatment dose, and CDC's prevention dose only for adults over 100 kg. Copying the sign without a clinician is how people take the wrong amount.

Vietnamese groups also pass around a schedule under the name thuốc chống shock độ cao (altitude-shock pills). In this context that name means acetazolamide, and the note says to buy that drug rather than a tonic sold under the same nickname. The schedule, as given, is:

- First dose 24 hours before departure.
- Then one tablet every 24 hours.
- Continue each day until the last day of the trip home, then stop.
- Drink about 2 litres of water a day while taking it. See [Water](#water).
- Stop at once if something feels wrong. Some people do not tolerate the ingredients.

The part that matches CDC is the start time: begin the day before ascent. The part that does not is the dose. "One tablet every 24 hours" never says how many milligrams are in the tablet. CDC prevention is 125 mg every 12 hours. The airport sign and the tourism advisory are 250 mg every 12 hours. Use the strength and the interval on your own prescription.

Stop the drug and get advice if you react to it. The effect CDC names as common is paresthesia (tingling), which is why their prevention dose is 125 mg. Headache, dizziness, nausea, queasiness, and flu-like fatigue are AMS. They are a reason to tell the guide. They are not proof that the pill disagrees with you, and they are not a reason to decide on your own to stop it.

### AMS, HAPE, and HACE

AMS often looks like an alcohol hangover. CDC's cardinal symptom is headache, usually with at least one of: loss of appetite, dizziness, fatigue, nausea, or vomiting. It can show up without headache, which is uncommon. Onset is usually 2 to 12 hours after arrival or after a further climb, often during or after the first night. It is easy to call this a cold, a bad night, or ordinary exhaustion. CDC's differential includes hangover, dehydration, exhaustion, and migraine. Unlike a viral illness, AMS does not bring a runny nose, fever, chills, or muscle aches. Cold medicine does not treat it. If you do not climb higher, it generally settles in 12 to 48 hours. Symptoms that start after 3 days at the same altitude, with no further ascent, should not be blamed on AMS.

Tell the guide or the group leader as soon as you feel a headache, fatigue, dizziness, nausea, or anything else that is off. Do not hide it.

Heavier signs need urgent care and usually a descent to a lower altitude:

- Breathlessness at rest, cough, chest congestion, or bloody, pink, or frothy sputum. CDC describes high-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE) as starting with congestion, cough, and unusual breathlessness on effort, then moving to breathlessness at rest, often with bloody sputum. Early HAPE can kill faster than brain edema.
- Confusion, loss of coordination, unusual drowsiness, or a drunken mental state. Those are the neurologic signs of high-altitude cerebral edema (HACE), which CDC treats as end-stage AMS. Coma can follow within a day if it is ignored.
- Symptoms that keep getting worse at the same altitude.

This note does not list treatment drugs for HAPE or HACE. Descent and oxygen matter more than a pill from a kit. In the field, the first move is to say so and go down.

### What a pulse oximeter does not decide

SpO2 is a supporting number. CDC says pulse oximetry in AMS is usually in the normal range for that altitude, or a little below it, and that a high saturation for the altitude seems protective. Healthy people also desaturate as they go up. At about 3,050 m, acute exposure can put arterial saturation around 88 to 91 percent. One reading does not mean you have AMS or that you do not. The diagnosis is the ascent plus the symptoms.

## Food to carry

A day can start very early and then spend hours on mountain roads. Outside Leh, do not count on a Vietnamese restaurant. Ladakh has food. The problem is the moment you are cold, tired, and hungry at once: altitude feels much worse. A little familiar food helps, especially for guests who do not yet eat local food.

Snacks for the vehicle:

- Chocolate.
- Ginger candy.
- Cơm cháy (crispy rice cracker).
- Biscuits or cake.
- Nuts.
- Any light snack you actually like.

Vietnamese food that travels:

- Instant mì.
- Instant bún, miến, or phở.
- Chà bông (pork floss).
- Dried dishes and instant soup packets.
- Vacuum-packed food, packed within airline and customs rules.

The meal notes under [Meals with a local guide](#meals-with-a-local-guide) are a separate point: Indian food cooked mild, without masala, was easy for one group once they tried it.

## Skin, lips, and nose

CDC notes that high altitude means cold, low humidity, stronger ultraviolet radiation, and lower air pressure. Dry, cracked skin and lips, a dry nose, or a light nosebleed are common. Eyes dry out in the same air.

Carry moisturizer, lip balm (kem môi), sunscreen, lubricating eye drops, and saline nasal drops or a rinse you already tolerate. A field packing note puts eye drops, nasal drops, and lip balm on the same list as the altitude drug, and says to buy those products rather than a substitute. Bring the drops and the balm you have already used at home.

## Photos at the high points

Ladakh makes people want to stop everywhere. Do not jump out at a high pass and spend the stop running, climbing, and posing. That matters at Khardung La, Pangong, Hanle, Umling La, and Tso Moriri, where the altitude and the weather press harder than at an ordinary viewpoint. Slow down. Breathe. Eat. Drink. Get warm. Then take the photo. The place will still be there.

## Weather

Read the forecast, and do not plan as if it were the day. Mountain conditions change with height, terrain, and hour. The useful habit is to be ready for several layers of weather in one day. That is why layering matters more than an app that says 10°C.

## Pace

"I am fit, so I can push" is a common mistake. CDC is direct: fitness does not affect the risk, and the two factors you can plan are the altitude where you sleep on night one and how fast that sleeping altitude rises after that. Above 3,000 m, the Wilderness Medical Society line that CDC cites is to raise sleeping altitude by no more than about 500 m a day, with an extra acclimatization day for every further 1,000 m. Going up faster raises the risk a lot. Hypoxemia is greatest during sleep, so a day trip that returns lower is easier on the body than sleeping at the high point.

A good Ladakh route is one the body can keep up with. It is not the route that ticks the most passes.

## If you remember three things

1. **Clothing.** Layers, warmth, a wind shell, and clothes you can move in. Shoes that handle rock and sand.
2. **The body.** Two slow days in Leh, steady water, symptoms reported early, and any altitude drug only as a doctor prescribed it.
3. **Food.** Backup calories, including food you already like.

## Motorbike

Riding Ladakh by motorbike is a strong experience for several reasons.

### Why a Motorbike

- **Adventure.** Ladakh has steep, spectacular pass roads such as Khardung La, Chang La, and Umling La (the highest motorable pass in the world, 5800 m). Riding them yourself feels like a challenge and a conquest.
- **Scenery.** A motorbike puts you in the wild landscape of the Himalaya: deep blue lakes such as Pangong Lake and Tso Moriri, and wide grasslands.
- **Freedom.** You can stop anywhere to take photos, rest, or look around small villages along the road, without the time limits of a car tour.
- **Culture.** Riding lets you meet local people and see Tibetan Buddhist culture at old monasteries such as Hemis, Thiksey, and Diskit.
- **The ride itself.** The Ladakh roads are among the finest in the world for bikers. Gravel, fast streams, and high passes make the trip memorable and leave a real sense of achievement.

### Practical Notes

- Checkpoints in these notes did not ask for an international driving permit. The 2023 rental shop photographed an English-language plastic licence (they write PET). What the riding note requires is real experience on a clutch bike. A step-through Honda is not that experience.
- For a foreign passport, Nubra, Pangong, Tso Moriri, Turtuk, and the eastern roads (Hanle, Umling La) need a Protected Area Permit, arranged through a registered agent in Leh. The riding note calls that paper an Inner Line Permit and says a land tour arranges it for Nubra, Pangong, and Tso Moriri. The 2023 pair called it a PAP and bought it in Leh Market. Guides read on 2026-10-06 still split the name by passport: a PAP for foreigners, and an environment fee rather than an ILP for Indian travellers. Leh town itself does not need it. The 2023 price, the photocopy count, and the checkpoint list are in [Ladakh Self-Guided Motorbike](/Travel/Destinations/International/Ladakh Self-Guided Motorbike).
- Carry motorcycle insurance, and travel insurance that includes medical care and rescue. That is the riding note's list. It does not name a policy.
- You need to be fit. Riding at altitude is harder than sitting on a bus. That does not cancel the CDC point that fitness does not protect you from altitude illness.
- Acclimatize before passes above 5,000 m. The riding note puts the region at about 3,500 m to 5,600 m, and its operator, Opr, keeps the first day in Leh as a rest day. The schedule to plan around is the [first 48 hours](#the-first-48-hours). Umling La, in [Why a Motorbike](#why-a-motorbike), is given as about 5,800 m, above the top of that range.
- The riding note's season is June to September, with days around 20°C and nights around 0°C. The 2023 pair rode in late September and early October and describe autumn, fares below peak summer, and cold they could live with.
- The surface changes. Good tarmac, then gravel, mud, and streams across the road.
- Before you leave, check brakes, tyres, lights, chain, and horn. Carry a spare chain if the route may meet snow, a spark plug, and a mini pump. Workshops on the road are scarce, and a breakdown with no backup gets expensive. The riding note wants a backup team. The 2023 pair had none, and they treat that as the risk of going self-guided.
- Fuel stations are sparse. The riding note fills up in Leh, Karu, and Diskit. Turtuk and Pangong had none on the 2023 loop. On a tour, ask the operator before you count on spare fuel.
- Bikes are easy to rent in Leh. If you have not ridden long mountain days, go with a group or hire a guide. Signal outside Leh is poor, and it is easy to get lost.
- Do not ride at night. It is cold, and the road is unlit.

Gear the riding note lists, some of it rentable with the bike:

- Full-face helmet, with a visor for dust and UV. The 2023 hire included two helmets. They took the full-face one, because the road is dusty and the wind is strong.
- A waterproof jacket that also keeps you warm. The wind shell and the high-cut shoes in [Clothing](#clothing) matter more on a bike than in a car.
- Thick gloves, riding pants, and high boots.
- Armor and limb protection, rentable in Leh.
- An offline map or a GPS. Do not plan on mobile data.
- A headache tablet and a bandage. Any altitude drug is the prescription in [Acetazolamide](#acetazolamide), not a dose copied from a riding list.

### How To Ride

- Keep the speed down, and leave a gap, especially around trucks.
- Do not brake hard on a descent or on gravel. The wheel can slide. On a long descent, stay in a low gear so the brakes are not doing all the work.
- Stop if breathing gets hard. Headache, dizziness, or nausea is the picture in [AMS, HAPE, and HACE](#ams-hape-and-hace). The riding note's instruction for those heavy signs is to lose altitude at once.
- India keeps left. A minute of home-side habit is how an oncoming crash happens. A good road captain keeps saying which lane you are in.
- Carry water and food you will actually eat. The list is under [Food to carry](#food-to-carry).

### The Roads The Riding Note Names

The note groups Leh, Khardung La, Nubra, and Umling La in one line. Those are not one road. Khardung La is the pass between Leh and Nubra. Umling La is on the eastern road toward Hanle.

- **Leh, Khardung La, Nubra.** Steep, narrow, and gravelly. Use a low gear on the way down so the brakes do not fade.
- **Leh to Pangong, over Chang La.** The note gives Chang La as 5,360 m, very steep, with snow possible. Ride slowly, stay off the cliff edge, and watch for falling rock.
- **Leh to Tso Moriri.** Empty. Few fuel stations and no phone. Leave with fuel, snacks, and water.

Shops, beds, and prices from the late-September 2023 loop are in [Ladakh Self-Guided Motorbike](/Travel/Destinations/International/Ladakh Self-Guided Motorbike).

### Himalayan 411 Or 450

The riding note's short list is a Royal Enfield in the 350 to 500 cc range, and it also names a KTM Duke. The Duke is that note's suggestion for power on a steep road. Nothing else here describes one being ridden across the gravel and the stream crossings. The bikes the 2023 pair actually found for rent were the Royal Enfield Classic 350 and the Himalayan 411. They call the Himalayan stronger, and also taller, with less room to strap luggage than the Classic 350's large rack.

The two Himalayans a Leh rental is likely to offer now are the 411 and the 450. Ladakh is a poor place to discover, halfway up a pass, that the bike does not match the rider. The roads in this note are passes above 5,000 m, loose sand, gravel, steep descents, washboard, and mud that appears after rain. The stretch from Pangong toward Hanle is the example used for the rough sections.

Published figures, from the [Times of India spec comparison](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/auto/bikes/new-royal-enfield-himalayan-450-vs-old-himalayan-411-specs-compared/articleshow/104903913.cms) (3 November 2023) and the [Financial Express sheet](https://www.financialexpress.com/auto/bike-news/royal-enfield-himalayan-old-vs-new-key-changes/3300718/) (8 November 2023), both read on 2026-10-06:

| | Himalayan 411 | Himalayan 450 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Engine | 411 cc, air- and oil-cooled single | 452 cc, liquid-cooled single |
| Output | about 24 hp at 6,500 rpm, 32 Nm at 4,000-4,500 rpm | about 40 PS at 8,000 rpm, 40 Nm at 5,500 rpm |
| Gearbox | 5-speed | 6-speed, slip-and-assist clutch |
| Front suspension | 41 mm conventional forks, 200 mm travel | 43 mm upside-down forks, 200 mm travel |
| Rear suspension | monoshock, 180 mm travel | linkage monoshock, 200 mm travel |
| Kerb weight | about 199 kg | about 196 kg |
| Seat height | 800 mm | about 825 mm, adjustable; a lower seat is an option |
| Fuel tank | about 15 litres | 17 litres |
| Instruments | analog-digital cluster | TFT, ride modes, Google Maps navigation |
| ABS | dual-channel, rear channel switchable | dual-channel, rear channel switchable |

The two sheets round the power figures slightly differently (24.3 hp or 24.8 PS; 39.5 hp or 40.02 PS). The gap is the point, so the table keeps the round numbers.

**Pull.** On the 411 the throttle feels dull at first, and the bike takes time to build speed. Once it is rolling it is steady, which suits a newer rider or a slow, deliberate pace. The 450 pulls harder from early in the throttle. On a long steep pass such as Khardung La or Chang La, the rider is less often pinned at full throttle in a low gear, and passing or carrying luggage is easier. Both engines lose power as the air thins. The 450 starts with more of it, which is why the extra pull shows up there.

**Suspension.** The 411's conventional forks are adequate, and they feel harsh if you carry speed on gravel. The 450 stays calmer on washboard, loose stone, and mud after rain. Front travel is 200 mm on both. What changes is the fork type, plus 20 mm more travel at the rear.

**Weight and who it suits.** A circulated line says the 411 weighs about 191 kg and the 450 is about 10 kg lighter, so the 450 is easier for a smaller rider or someone new to a big bike. The published kerb weights are about 199 kg and 196 kg. A few kilograms do not decide the bike. The 450's standard seat is also taller, about 825 mm against 800 mm, which matters more when a foot has to reach the ground. A rental with the low seat narrows that gap. The 411 does feel planted, and it is a heavy machine to pick up after a fall. The 450 turns more willingly. More power and a taller seat still make the 411 the calmer bike for someone who wants a predictable pace.

**Which to take.** Take the 411 for a steady, simple trip. Take the 450 when you want the extra pull and the calmer suspension on rough ground, and you are comfortable with the taller seat and the stronger engine. The simpler 411, with fewer electronics, is the one this note trusts for fewer small faults. That is a rider's impression, not a breakdown survey. Either bike still depends on the acclimatization, spare fuel, and backup plan above. The 450's larger tank is the only spec here that changes how often you look for fuel.

## Meals with a local guide

In India people eat with their hands. Indian food smells strong, and it is hard to eat if you have not been there. You will not understand until you go.

Indian food cooked together with their guide, Gyus, was actually delicious. He was very caring. Near mealtime, from the car, he kept calling the homestay to dictate a separate way of cooking for the group, always stressing "no masala, no spicy". They are 8 women who eat easily, so besides finishing the dishes Gyus ordered, they tried some real Indian food meant for Gyus and the driver, kept saying "good good", and after that ate fully Indian food and found it very good.

## First day in Leh

They were told to walk lightly, speak softly, and smile gently to avoid altitude sickness. The advice fit because the land tour lodged them at a resort about 3 km from the market, on a slightly remote road. The resort looked like a fairy-tale castle: apple trees heavy with fruit, fragrant yellow apricot clusters in the wind, unusual brightly colored flowers planted neatly along the paths under tall sturdy birch trees, and beds of many green vegetables farther off. The writer's room was small and looked onto the apricot garden. On day one she only did two things: say "Hi Gyus, I need a car to market" and pick apricots until she was full.

## Homestays

Gyus specifically chose homestays meant for local people, a bit farther and quieter, but small, pretty, and the hosts very thoughtful. In Zanskar they stayed 3 nights. The host was lovely: every morning and evening she carefully set food in a cozy dining room decorated in the traditional style, warm and charming. The food was very good; dessert was a beautiful, very good fresh-fruit sweet soup. When the food on each plate got low she walked around with the pot, smiling, saying "more more". That smile is what made them gain weight after the days there. Other homestays were like the Zanskar host, "more more" the whole time, and the writer saw how happy their faces were when the group was genuinely relaxed with the real Indian food cooked for them.

## Limit

These health notes are for planning a trip. They are not medical advice. Anyone with a chronic illness, a previous altitude illness, or a route that sleeps very high should talk with a doctor before going.

Ladakh is the high Himalaya, Tibetan Buddhist culture, and a hard climate, not only Nubra, Pangong, Khardung La, and Umling La. Go slowly, prepare, listen to the body, and do not travel only for the photos.
