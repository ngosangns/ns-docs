## The manifesto

[Manifesto for testing in the age of AI](https://thefutureofsoftwaretesting.com/manifesto/) is marked "Published September 2026" and signed by all four. The line they repeat is "AI accelerates testing. People lead quality." The page says more code, more tests, and faster delivery do not by themselves make better software. Five commitments follow. This is a paraphrase of the headings, not the essay:

1. Earn confidence. A green check and a large test count are not evidence. Look at what was tested, assumed, and left out, and whether that evidence matches the business risk.
2. Keep people accountable. A model can generate code, tests, and analysis. It cannot own what ships. Calling quality everyone's job does not make everyone an experienced tester.
3. Change the craft, not freeze the old mechanics. The page expects some repetitive work, and some roles built on it, to go away. It also says manual exploration, deterministic automation, and autonomous testing should sit side by side, chosen by risk rather than by fashion.
4. Give the model context, then check the model. The list they treat as the new testing infrastructure is requirements, source, test cases, results, production behavior, architecture, boundaries, constraints, and domain knowledge. They name hallucination, inconsistency, bias, omission, and false confidence as failure modes of the model itself.
5. Judge testing by the product, not by testing activity. Besides functional correctness they name usable, accessible, secure, resilient, performant, and worthy of trust.

The closing call says the future of testing should not be set by AI vendors, defended by people protecting the past, or left to machines.

Article 01 is labeled "the series opener", by Alex Martins, "5 min read". The document title is "Who owns software quality in the age of AI?", which is not the H1. The piece restates the manifesto line, then asks who validates model output, who accepts the risk, and who is accountable for what reaches customers. A figure caption says AI magnifies the process it enters: strong practices scale, and weak coverage scales too. The closer still says "this month" the series will start exploring those questions. That sentence is from a 30 September page. By this check, article 02 had already been published and 03 and 04 were still upcoming.

Article 02 is "9 min read". Its document title is "Is manual testing dead? What AI really changes for testers". The header does not name a single author. The body is a conversation among the four. The opening provocation, which the article says nobody fully accepted, is that manual testing, automation, and everything else in testing are dead because AI will take over. The argument they keep:

- The work moving first is mechanical: scripted checks, repeated regression, routine generation, maintenance, and result sorting. Mush Honda's line in the piece is that the repeatable layer goes away and judgment stays.
- Cristiano Caetano's bottleneck story: manual execution was automated, automation then created brittle suites and noisy results, and AI is now speeding those up too. The decision that remains is whether to release.
- Vu Lam's line is that running a test someone else already wrote is the easy job, and the easy job is also the easy job for a model.
- Alex Martins's observation in the piece is that many large enterprises still have a lot of manual testing, often because those people know the domain.
- Three capabilities they say get more valuable: judgment (what the evidence means), domain expertise (a plausible output can still be wrong), and accountability (Mush: "You can't blame the AI"). They also say a model scales the process you already have, including a weak one.
- The Monday section says to onboard the model as a teammate that still needs context, not as a takeover. Vu: "AI is a member of the team, not a takeover of the team."
- The ending, in Vu's words as printed: the easy part of manual testing is dead; manual testing in another form is not, because the model has limits. What they say is disappearing is pre-scripted, repetitive, low-judgment execution.
